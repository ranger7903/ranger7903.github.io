🗓️ The night before something important

On an important day, what goes wrong is almost always a question you hadn’t thought about. So the night before, instead of memorising what you’ll say, write the five questions you least want to hear and give each a two-sentence answer.

What the real problem is

The night before, we polish our own side: memorise the lines, fix the slides, rehearse in the mirror. But on the day it goes wrong when the other side asks something you didn’t prepare for. Rehearse a hundred times and one unexpected question still shakes you.

What Kobe Bryant did

Kobe Bryant was famous for studying opponents relentlessly on film — not just their plays, but which way they turned when tired and how they reacted to referees. His own shooting practice was a constant; what he studied fresh before a game was the other side.

The one line

The night before is not for getting better — you can’t in one day. It is for knowing what they will ask.

One hour, four steps

1. Write three lines on what they are worried about

Interviewer → If I hire someone who leaves soon, I'm the one blamed Supplier → If delivery is late, I've nothing to report upstairs Parent meeting→ What the teacher really thinks of my child

Know their worry and their questions become obvious.

2. Write the five questions you least want to hear

· Why did you leave your last job after a year? · At this price, isn't someone else cheaper? · So how many people actually use it now? · Why should it be you? · What will you do if this fails?

Not wanting to write them is normal. The ones you don’t want to write are tomorrow’s questions.

3. Two sentences each — first admit, then what you did

Q: Why did you leave after a year? ✗ “The company was having a hard time…” (sounds like an excuse) ○ “It wasn't the work I'd been told I'd be doing, so I didn't last. This time I spoke to three people in this role before applying.” ↑ admit ↑ what I did about it

Admitting instead of excusing makes you sound stronger, not weaker — because it puts the listener at ease.

4. Read them aloud once

Out loud, not in your head. Words that don’t fit your mouth show up at once — swap them for plain ones. A question you have read aloud once won’t shake you tomorrow: it has become a question you’ve already thought about.

Three common mistakes

  • Learning something new the night before — you won’t improve in a day, you’ll only sleep less.
  • Practising the comfortable questions — it feels good and helps nothing tomorrow.
  • Memorising answers word for word — memorised speech sounds memorised. Keep the two-sentence skeleton; find the words on the day.

The last thing that night

Close the sheet and go to bed early. Five written, read aloud — the night’s work is done. More looking only feeds the nerves. In the morning, glance over the five lines once and walk in.
