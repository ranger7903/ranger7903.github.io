🧩 When nothing you read sticks

Good ideas vanish within a week not because your memory is poor but because there is nowhere to hang them. Write down the things you wonder about as questions, and hang what you read on those.

What the real problem is

A book, a talk, a good conversation — a week later it’s gone. “That was good… what was it?” Without hooks, clothes pile up on the floor however many you buy.

Feynman’s “twelve favourite problems”

The physicist Richard Feynman is said to have kept a dozen problems in mind at all times. Whenever he heard something new, he checked whether it caught on one of them. When it did, what was a passing remark for everyone else became a clue for him.

To be honest about it

There is no record of Feynman writing such a list down. The story comes from his colleague, the mathematician Gian-Carlo Rota. Read it as “this helps,” not “Feynman told us to.” The number twelve doesn’t matter — five or eight is fine. Writing it down is what matters.

Four steps for tonight

1. Write questions, not topics

✗ Health ○ What do people who still walk without knee pain at 80 do differently? ✗ Parenting ○ What words make a child want to do something on their own? ✗ Business ○ Why do some shops get regulars and others don't?

It has to end in a question mark. The question mark is the hook.

2. Keep it between five and twelve

Three catches nothing; twenty is the same as none. Start with five and add as you go.

3. Keep it where you will see it

First page of a notebook, top of your phone notes, above your desk — somewhere you see it at least once a week. In a drawer it doesn’t exist.

4. After anything you read or watch, ask one thing

“Which of my questions does this hang on?” Hangs → one line under that question (and where you saw it) Doesn't → let it go. This matters more.

Letting go of what doesn’t hang is half of it. Trying to keep everything is why nothing stays.

What happens after a month

What you getWhat it feels like
It accumulatesAbout ten lines under one question becomes your own view, not someone else’s
You see what you can skipA book that hangs on none of your questions is a book you needn’t read. Time comes back
You notice different thingsSame story, different hook — which is where “how did you think of that?” comes from

Three common mistakes

  • Writing grand questions — “What is the meaning of life?” catches nothing. Write what you actually wonder about this month.
  • Trying to answer them — it isn’t a list of things to solve; it’s a net. Years without an answer are fine.
  • Never changing it — look every six months. Cross out questions that have gone stale; new ones fill the space.

Five to start from

1. Why do some people leave me energised after thirty minutes, and others drained? 2. Where does money leak most in our home? 3. How do people who keep going start differently? 4. What do I say to my child that would be better left unsaid? 5. Do the things I put off have something in common?

Not to copy — just to show the size.
