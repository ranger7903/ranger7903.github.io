⚖️ Stuck on a big decision

If you have circled the same decision for days, the problem may not be that the answer is hard — it may be that you have no way to weigh it. Six boxes on one sheet of paper will usually give you a direction today.

Why thinking harder doesn’t settle it

Weighed only in your head, it goes round in circles: yes in the morning, no by evening, because the day’s mood rocks the scale. And most of us weigh the wrong thing. We weigh “what if it goes wrong?” — when what hurts most later is often “I never tried.”

The question Jeff Bezos used

In 1994 Jeff Bezos was deciding whether to leave a secure job to start what became Amazon. He later described his method in interviews: imagine you are eighty, looking back. Which path would you regret least?

He did not weigh the chance of failure. He weighed how eighty-year-old him would feel about never having tried — and says the answer came quickly. It is usually called regret minimisation: not a way to compute odds, but a way to choose the side that will hurt less later.

Six boxes on paper

Getting it out of your head and onto paper is the whole trick. Written down, it stops wobbling.

1. What exactly are you deciding — in one sentence

“Should I leave my job and start my own business?”

“How should I live?” is too big. Shrink it to something with a yes or no answer.

2. Eighty-year-old you looks back — two lines

Tried, and it didn't work: “Well, I tried. I was terrified at the time.” Never tried: “I wish I'd given it a go.”

Put the two side by side and see which one hurts more.

3. Can it be undone?

  • Reversible (you could get a job again, cancel the contract) → try it sooner
  • Irreversible (all your savings, selling the house) → slowly, and find out more first

4. If the worst happens, what happens to me — in numbers

Worst case: not established within a year. Eight months of savings gone. After that, I'd need to find a job again.

Vague fear feels huge. Written in numbers, it is usually smaller than you thought.

5. What would make the decision easier to know?

To find out: (1) talk to two people who have done it (2) add up what the first six months would cost

Often you can’t decide not for lack of courage but because something is unknown. Write it down and the decision becomes a list of things to find out.

6. By when will you decide?

Decision day: 15 November. Open this sheet again with both questions answered.

Without a date, “thinking about it” lasts for years. Put a day in the calendar.

And the most important thing

Keep the sheet. Six months or a year later, add one line: “did it turn out as I expected?” After a few of these you will see which way you tend to be wrong — “I always make the worst case too big” — and the next decision gets easier.

It works for small things too

The questionSeen from eighty
Go to this gathering or not?At eighty you won’t remember → whichever is easier
Message this person first?It will keep nagging if you don’t → message
Take the expensive course?See whether “never learning it” bothers you more than the money

If eighty-year-old you won’t even remember it, it isn’t worth the worry. Knowing that alone frees up a lot of time.
