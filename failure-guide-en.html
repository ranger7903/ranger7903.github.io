🧱 Not making the same mistake twice

We trip in the same place six months later because what we learned had nowhere to stay. Here is how to turn something that went wrong into one line you can use next time, in five boxes and fifteen minutes.

Why the same mistake comes back

When something goes wrong we usually do one of two things: cover it up, or find out whose fault it was. Neither helps next time. Cover it and you forget; blame and everyone hides. Not because people are bad — because there is no place for the lesson to live.

Ray Dalio’s one line

Ray Dalio, who built one of the world’s largest hedge funds, wrote in Principles:

Pain + Reflection = Progress

Pain on its own does not make anyone better. Looking at the pain does. What he actually did was modest: each time something went wrong, he wrote it down and turned it into a rule he could use next time. The collection later became the book.

Five boxes, fifteen minutes

The day after is best. A week later, memory has already shifted to the comfortable version.

1. What happened — facts only

Bad: The contact was sloppy, so delivery was late. Good: Material due 12 Sep arrived 19 Sep. Delivery moved 20 Sep → 27 Sep.

2. What did I know at the time?

Not what you know now — only what you had then. Skip this and all that is left is useless self-blame: “how did I not know?”

Knew then: I'd heard they were busy. Didn't ask how busy.

3. Where did it turn — exactly one place

Turning point: on 5 Sep I didn't ask “when can you send it?”

4. One line for next time — “When …, I …”

This box is the whole point. Write a rule, not a feeling.

Bad: Be more careful next time. Good: When I hand work to someone else, I ask them to name the date they can deliver, there and then.

“Do better” changes nothing. A rule has when, what and how in it.

5. Where will this rule live?

Written and filed, it won’t be read. Put it where the situation happens again: a line in your email template, your start-of-job checklist, a calendar reminder.

In a team — just two rules

  • No names in the rules. Not “make sure Sam isn’t late” but “when handing over work, have the other person name the date.” The moment names appear, everyone hides.
  • Delete rules as they pile up. Past thirty, nobody reads them. Twice a year, remove the ones nobody uses.

On your own — once a month is plenty

Writing every time wears you out. Write only when you feel “hang on, this happened before.” That feeling is the sign it is repeating. Ten lines a year is a lot: the shapes of our mistakes are fewer than we think.
